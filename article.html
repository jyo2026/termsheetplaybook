Building a Legal Research Tool for Indian Term Sheets

Why generic AI tools get Indian law wrong, how a RAG-based term sheet playbook was built in two weeks, and the technical architecture that makes it work.

JN
Jyotirmoy Nath
Corporate Lawyer · 26 June 2026 · 8 min read

Background

As AI absorbs routine junior work, legal associates are losing the learning that came with doing the foundational work themselves. Junior associates no longer get the opportunity to mark up term sheets, but they are still on calls and in negotiations. They are arriving without the preparation that marking up a term sheet used to force. That prior engagement with the document was where the real learning happened. Without it, observation replaces understanding.

The tool

A RAG-based legal research tool for Indian PE/VC term sheet negotiations, grounded in Companies Act 2013 and FEMA legislation. Junior associates can browse key clauses like liquidation preference, anti-dilution and drag-along, or query specific negotiating positions and get structured, India-specific answers covering founder and investor counsel perspectives, market practice, and red flags. The playbook handles the foundation and the lawyer handles everything above it.

How it was built

I built this in two weeks on the free version of Claude. No engineering background, no budget. I worked in sessions timed around the usage limits, starting at 8am, then again around 2pm, then in the evening.

The work ran in two parallel threads: one for the knowledge base, researching, curating, and encoding how Indian term sheet clauses are actually negotiated; and one for the front end, building an interface where the information is easy to find and read. Debugging happened by taking screenshots and dropping them into Claude chat.

But the technical choices matter more than the timeline. Here is what shaped the architecture:

Stack: Next.js + OpenAI API + Structured Database

Frontend: Next.js and React with TypeScript. This runs on Vercel with zero DevOps overhead.

Backend: Next.js API routes. API calls to OpenAI stay server-side, so no client-side API keys are exposed in the browser.

Knowledge Base: Around 50 Indian PE/VC clause chunks structured as TypeScript objects, not vectors. Each chunk has title, keywords, legal flags, risk level, founder/investor positions, and market practice.

LLM: OpenAI's gpt-4o-mini. A smaller model works here because retrieval is deterministic. The LLM only answers from the chunks passed to it, no independent research required.

Technical Architecture: Why These Choices Matter

A RAG system has three stages: retrieval, ranking, and generation. Most implementations spend cycles optimising for semantic similarity through embeddings and cosine distance. This system took a different path.

Structured data over vectors

The knowledge base is a TypeScript object array. Each clause chunk has explicit fields: title, keywords, legalFlag, marketStandard, risk level (critical/high/medium/low). This structure means every field can be used for retrieval and filtering.

Why not embeddings? Because a lawyer asking "Is this market standard?" needs a deterministic answer, not a fuzzy similarity score. A lawyer also needs to see why the system surfaced a given clause. Embeddings are a black box. Keyword scores are transparent.

Keyword-based search with scoring

When a lawyer queries "liquidation," the retrieval function scores clauses on:

  • Title match (+10 points): "Liquidation Preference" scores highest.
  • Keyword match (+5 points): Pre-indexed keywords like "waterfall," "preferred," "1x," "payout" boost relevance.
  • Legal flag match (+3 points): This clause surfaces statutory context. The lp-002 legalFlag references Section 43 and Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 9 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules 2014, which is the operative provision governing the issue of participating preference shares, and requires corresponding Articles of Association amendments.
    • Indian law on this point is governed by the pricing guidelines under Rule 21(2)(c) of the NDI Rules 2019. A put option providing an absolute guaranteed exit price higher than FMV at the time of exercise violates those guidelines. A capped-IRR structure is permissible provided the payout at exercise does not exceed FMV at that point. The valuation must be certified by a SEBI-registered Merchant Banker or a Chartered Accountant.
  • Risk level boost (+1–2 points): Critical and high-risk clauses rank higher because lawyers need to see them first.

The top 5 matches are passed to the LLM as context. Latency is sub-100ms. A lawyer asking "Is put option enforceable in India?" gets the FEMA NDI Rules constraint immediately because it is tagged in the legalFlag field. No embeddings, no API calls, no latency.

Next.js for fast deployment

The backend is a Next.js API route: a single endpoint that takes a query and mode (founder or investor) and returns a JSON response with structured advice. Vercel deploys this with sub-100ms cold start. When a lawyer clicks "Ask," they get a response in under 2 seconds.

Barrier to adoption drops when tools do not make you wait. If the tool took 5 seconds per query, junior associates would use ChatGPT instead. Speed forces good architecture.

Role-based system prompts

The LLM gets two different system prompts depending on mode:

Founder counsel mode: "You are a senior corporate lawyer at Trilegal acting as FOUNDER COUNSEL for a Seed or Series A funding round in India. Your job is to protect the founders' interests..."

Investor counsel mode: "You are a senior corporate lawyer at Trilegal acting as INVESTOR COUNSEL for a Seed or Series A funding round in India. Your job is to protect the investor's interests..."

The same clause chunk returns different negotiating advice depending on perspective. A liquidation preference is flagged as "critical risk" in founder counsel mode and "acceptable baseline" in investor counsel mode. This trains junior associates to think like both sides of the table, which is exactly what real negotiation requires.

Why RAG (And Not Just General AI)

Ask ChatGPT "Is a put option enforceable in Indian term sheets?" and it draws on training data from UK case law, US contract law, and generic fintech guidance. The answer is plausible but wrong. Indian law on this point is governed by the pricing guidelines under Rule 21(2)(c) of the NDI Rules 2019. A put option providing an absolute guaranteed exit price higher than FMV at the time of exercise violates those guidelines. A capped-IRR structure is permissible provided the payout at exercise does not exceed FMV at that point. The valuation must be certified by a SEBI-registered Merchant Banker or a Chartered Accountant. A generic AI tool citing UK or US precedent gets none of this right.

Generic tools guess. RAG systems retrieve and acknowledge when they do not know.

The Playbook works differently. When queried, it:

  • Retrieves the relevant clause chunk on put options, which is tagged with references to Rule 21(2)(c) of the NDI Rules 2019 and the dual-track drafting requirement for mixed foreign and domestic investor rounds.
  • Passes only that chunk as context to the LLM.
  • The LLM generates an answer grounded in that chunk, with citations to Indian law.
  • If the query falls outside the knowledge base, the system returns "not covered in our knowledge base" instead of hallucinating.

That honest boundary matters. A tool that acknowledges its limits is more useful to a practitioner than one that confidently answers questions outside its scope.

Limitations: What This Architecture Trades Off

This design prioritises speed and transparency over breadth. The knowledge base is hand-curated, which means:

Strength: High quality for what is covered. Every clause is vetted against Indian law and market practice. When the system has an answer, it is accurate and grounded.

Weakness: Gaps exist. If a clause type is not in the database, say a novel instrument introduced post-June 2026 or a highly specialised tax structure, the system returns "not covered." Scaling would require either manual curation by domain experts, which is sustainable but slow, or a hybrid approach where core clauses are maintained manually, ancillary content is auto-ingested, and RAG is used for novelty detection. That is considerably more complex to build.

For now, the tool is intentionally narrow: term sheet clauses only, not SHA/SSA/CPA mechanics, not M&A precedent, not regulatory compliance checklists. Narrowness prevents hallucination. Breadth without depth is where RAG systems fail.

What comes next

The constraints here were deliberate. Free tools, no budget, two weeks. The knowledge base is limited to what I could research and encode alone.

A version built on a firm's institutional knowledge would look different: more clauses, more depth on multi-investor scenarios, integration with SHA templates and precedent documentation, and usage analytics to identify gaps and friction points.

But this version proves the core point. Junior associates do not need AI to replace lawyers. They need AI to handle the foundation, the retrieval, the structure, the India-specific legal context, so they can focus on judgment, negotiation, and the relationships that matter.
